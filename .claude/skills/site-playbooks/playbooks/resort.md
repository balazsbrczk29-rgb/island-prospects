# Playbook: hotel, resort, inn, lodge, beach club, guesthouse

## 1. The business and the buyer
From Nassau boutique hotels to Out Island cottages and fishing lodges. The buyer is a couple or family from the
US or Canada planning months ahead on a phone, comparing your rooms with Booking and Expedia listings, and with
famous resorts they have seen on Instagram. Anglers for lodges; wedding and group planners for some. They fear:
the photos lied, it is hard to reach, it is overpriced. They choose the place that makes them feel the morning
there: this beach, this light, this room, this quiet.

## 2. What wins the owner
"Guests book you directly because your site makes them feel the place, instead of paying the OTAs 15-25%."
Many island properties have dated sites with small photos and no clear way to ask about dates.

## 3. Big ideas
- **A day on the cay.** A ScrollStage that moves through one real day: first light on the beach, breakfast,
  the boat, the bar at sunset, the night sky. Each chapter a real photograph made alive with PhotoDepth (or the
  Ocean recipe for open water), with a single line of copy. (ScrollStage, PhotoDepth, Ocean)
- **Wake up here.** The hero is the view from the room or the beach at their real orientation (does it face
  sunrise or sunset?), slowly breathing; the room selector is a HorizontalTrack of their real rooms.
- **The journey is part of it.** For remote islands: a cinematic route from the airport or ferry to the door
  (flight, boat, golf cart), each step a real photo or scene, ending at the room. Turns "remote" into romance.
- **The letter.** For small family inns: the site reads as a warm letter from the owners, with photographs
  laid in like prints. Personal and rare.
- **Film first.** If they have a good reel: a calm looping film hero, then a quiet editorial page. (ScrollVideo)

## 4. The page
The feeling (big idea), the place in two sentences, the rooms (each real room type: name, view, bed, sleeps,
from their site; HorizontalTrack or a refined list), eating and drinking, what to do (only real nearby places),
getting here (airport code, ferry, transfer), dates enquiry, contact.

## 5. Imagery and 3D
Their own photos are gold: crop them bigger and better than they ever have. Commons for the island's real
beaches and landmarks. Lovable only for atmosphere details that never claim to be their rooms (linen in morning
light, a drink on a rail, louvres and sea light). Ocean for open-water moments. Use the photographs the idea needs.

## 6. Interactions
Dates enquiry (dates, guests, room, a note: Enquiry, to WhatsApp or email, or their real booking engine);
room comparison; a sunrise and sunset indicator for their beach if orientation is known.

## 7. Motion
Unhurried. Slow crossfades, a gentle breathing parallax, long eases. Nothing pops.

## 8. Type and colour
Warm serif or elegant humanist display with a quiet sans. Colour from their place: sand, sea glass, conch pink,
casuarina green, shutter colours; light pages, one deep section for evening. Never generic "luxury gold".

## 9. Voice
Sensory and precise, never brochure clichés ("paradise", "nestled", "oasis" are banned). Tone example, write
your own: "The sea is twelve steps from your door. Mornings here are very quiet."

## 10. Conversion
Primary: ask about dates. Secondary: call or WhatsApp. If they have a real booking engine, it is the primary
button. Rates only when sourced; otherwise "Rates on request".

## 11. SEO
`Hotel`, `Resort` or `LodgingBusiness` (`BedAndBreakfast` for inns), `amenityFeature`, `checkinTime` only if
sourced, `containsPlace` for the restaurant. Queries: "<island> hotel", "beach cottages <island> Bahamas".

## 12. References
aman.com (photography first, almost no interface), sixsenses.com (place-led calm), belmond.com (editorial
storytelling), thebrando.com (island romance), habitas.com (warm, human).

## 13. Traps
Fake rates; generic beach photos that are not their beach; a booking widget that pretends to work; card grids of
amenities with icons; testimonials without a source; "paradise".

## 14. Done looks like
A guest feels the morning there within five seconds, understands each room, knows how to get there, and asks
about dates in two taps.
