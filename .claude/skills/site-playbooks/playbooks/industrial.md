# Playbook: industrial and B2B (ports, shipyards, fuel and energy, brewing, distribution, heavy industry)

## 1. The business and the buyer
The big operators of the islands: container ports, ship repair yards, fuel and energy groups, breweries, food
distributors, asphalt plants. Buyers: shipping lines, procurement teams, government, developers, investors,
future employees. They fear: capacity that is not real, safety and compliance gaps, a partner that cannot scale.
They choose an operator that shows scale, precision and reliability with evidence.

## 2. What wins the owner
"Your site finally shows the scale of what you do, the way global peers present themselves." These firms often
have dated corporate pages; the pitch is pride and investor-grade presentation. Usually priority D or C: build
after SMEs.

## 3. Big ideas
- **The operation from above.** A scroll-driven flyover of the real operation (a photo sequence or Commons aerial
  made alive with PhotoDepth, or a 3D site model with ScrollStage and CameraPath): berths, cranes, tanks, plant,
  each labelled with a sourced capability.
- **The chain.** For energy or distribution groups: one continuous scroll that follows the product from ship to
  tank to truck to the light switch or shelf, each stage a real photograph and a sourced fact.
- **Scale made human.** Huge machines photographed at human scale (a worker beside a crane, a hull in dry dock),
  with numbers only where sourced, set large like a data poster.
- **The capability sheet.** For engineering buyers: an elegant technical document page (capabilities, dry dock
  dimensions, certifications, contacts per desk), with one immense photograph per section.

## 4. The page
The scale (big idea), what they do (real divisions or services), capabilities and specifications (sourced),
safety and certifications (sourced), projects or clients (sourced), careers if relevant, contacts per desk,
location and access.

## 5. Imagery and 3D
Their own photography first; Commons aerials of the real port or yard; Lovable for process details and atmosphere
(blue-hour operations, machinery at work at human scale), labelled illustrative. 3D only when it reads as real
engineering (accurate proportions, real materials), never as a toy port.

## 6. Interactions
A capability explorer (choose a vessel size, see whether the dock fits, if sourced); a request-for-quote or
enquiry routed to the right desk (Enquiry with a desk choice); a filterable project list.

## 7. Motion
Heavy and deliberate: slow camera, weighty easing, overlays that draw like engineering lines.

## 8. Type and colour
A precise grotesk, possibly with a technical mono for data. Colour from steel, concrete, sea and the brand colour.
Data set large. Never neon, never sci-fi.

## 9. Voice
Factual, confident, international. Numbers carry the story only when sourced. Tone example, write your own:
"Three dry docks. Ships up to cruise-liner size. Twenty-four hours a day."

## 10. Conversion
Primary: contact the right desk (commercial, operations, careers). Secondary: call. Clear addresses and hours.

## 11. SEO
`Corporation` or the specific type, `areaServed`, `department` for desks. Queries: "<service> Freeport",
"ship repair Bahamas", "fuel supplier Bahamas".

## 12. References
maersk.com (global logistics clarity), arup.com (engineering storytelling), fosterandpartners.com (projects as
photography), portofrotterdam.com (a port presented at scale).

## 13. Traps
Toy 3D ports; invented capacities; stock-like generic industry photos; walls of corporate text; ten equal
service cards.

## 14. Done looks like
A shipping line or investor understands the scale and capability in five seconds and knows exactly whom to
contact.
